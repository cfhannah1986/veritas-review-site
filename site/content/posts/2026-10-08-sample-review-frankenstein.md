# A Critical Review of *Frankenstein*

### Opening Assessment

The narrative opens with an arresting epistolary frame in the desolate polar sea, establishing a haunting dynamic between ambitious explorer Walton and the ruined Victor Frankenstein. By grounding the tragic tale in Walton's letters and immediately introducing the chilling pursuit across the ice, the work powerfully frames its core meditation on intellectual hubris and isolation. In these opening sequences, the reader encounters startling visions of a "gigantic being" seen upon the vast frozen waste, followed closely by the rescue of a hollowed-out survivor pursuing him upon a "drifted sledge." The sight of this strange figure having the "shape of a man" sets an eerie, anticipatory mood. Through Walton's initial correspondence, the text constructs a clear thematic parallel: Walton's dangerous polar ambitions reflect Victor's unchecked scientific passions, serving as a cautionary vessel before the main confession even begins.

### Strengths

Among the work's foremost triumphs is its brilliant synthesis of psychological gothic horror and philosophical allegory in the creature's developmental journey and tragic socialization. When the creature takes command of the narration to recount his early months, the text departs from simple monster tropes to stage an intricate philosophical experiment. The creature's moral and intellectual education is rendered with astonishing pathos as he watches the cottagers and eavesdrops on lessons surrounding Volney's *Ruins of Empires*. His emotional development through observing the "sweet Arabian" Safie allows him to comprehend history, virtue, and language, transforming him into an articulate subject rather than a mindless beast. His growing self-awareness carries genuine existential horror, particularly when he perceives his own monstrous visage in a "reflection in a transparent pool" and measures his solitary state against John Milton, reading *Paradise Lost as a literal true history*. This philosophical scaffolding imbues his plight with unforgettable tragic gravity.

Furthermore, the manuscript demonstrates masterful atmospheric setting choices that vividly externalize inner torment and cosmic alienation. The reader is continually pulled into stark, unforgiving environments that mirror the characters' fractured psyches. Whether depicting Walton's vessel "enclosed by ice amid thick fog" or the sublime, terrifying expanses of the "sea of ice" where creator and creation confront one another, the physical world acts as an extension of spiritual ruin. The very moment of unnatural life being infused into dead flesh is indelibly rendered on a "dreary night in November," matching cold rain and dying embers to the catastrophic realization of Victor's arrogance. These gothic backdrops provide profound psychological resonance to every major confrontation.

### Weaknesses

Despite these profound thematic achievements, the narrative suffers from an over-reliance on extraordinary contrivances and convenient coincidences to distribute exposition and propel key events. Rather than allowing character choices or rigorous causal mechanics to advance the narrative, pivotal revelations rely on sheer fortune. The creature's intellectual leap is facilitated by stumbling upon an abandoned "leathern portmanteau in the woods" that happens to contain foundational literary masterpieces, while his understanding of his own genesis occurs only because he inadvertently pockets the creator's exact "journal of the four months" from the Ingolstadt laboratory. A similar reliance on chance occurs when the creature happens upon a sleeping Justine Moritz and slips the portrait into a fold of her dress to frame her out of malice. These fortuitous accidents detract from the otherwise rigorous psychological realism, calling attention to the mechanical gears of plotting.

Additionally, Victor's recurring paralysis and self-serving secrecy under pressure directly enable catastrophic fallout while straining reader sympathy. When innocent Justine stands accused of William's murder, Victor repeatedly shrinks from intervention "for fear of being thought insane." While he laments in private that "I was the true murderer," his silence persists even as Justine faces "threats of excommunication and hellfire" that force her into an unjust admission of guilt. Her eventual revelation that her "confession was a lie" highlights the deadly cost of Victor's cowardice. By prioritizing his own social reputation over an innocent girl's life, Victor's moral failure risks alienating the reader, undermining the balance between tragic victimhood and unforgivable negligence.

### Character Assessment

Victor is a compelling study of monomaniacal obsession alternating with debilitating guilt and evasive self-pity. When his scientific labors bear fruit, his obsessive zeal collapses into instantaneous "horror and disgust," instigating an abandonment that defines the rest of the text. His inability to shoulder moral duty is matched by his frantic retreat from reality whenever consequences arrive. Withholding critical evidence "for fear of being thought insane" while wallowing in the secret belief that "I was the true murderer," Victor embodies a deeply flawed archetype whose grand ambitions mask a fundamental moral frailty.

In poignant contrast stands the creature, whose articulate suffering as an outcast yearning for connection forms the beating heart of the book. Denied basic tenderness and subjected to unprovoked violence, his descent into vengeance feels terrifyingly earned. His eventual proclamation of "everlasting war against humanity" is not the instinct of an unthinking brute, but the calculated response of an abused rational mind that has been permanently rejected by its maker and society alike. This dynamic creates an enduring, agonizing dialectic between two ruined entities bound by mutual resentment.

### Narrative Structure and Pacing

The novel employs a complex Chinese-box structure that frames the narrative with remarkable dexterity. Moving from Walton's framing letters into Victor's autobiographical chronicle, and finally into the creature's core developmental memoir, the text layers its perspectives with architectural care. Walton records these events as Victor implores him to "listen to my tale," creating an intimate oral storytelling dynamic anchored by Walton's promise to "write down the story each night."

In terms of pacing, the narrative momentum purposefully decelerates during the central section at the Mer de Glace to prioritize an extended philosophical study of language, education, and moral sympathy through the cottagers' domestic life. This deceleration offers extensive space for the "history of the De Lacey family," tracing the creature's intellectual awakening via Volney's *Ruins of Empires* and the arrival of the "sweet Arabian." While this shift halts the visceral momentum established in Geneva, it enriches the narrative by balancing raw gothic dread with rich social commentary.

### Prose and Voice

The prose displays rich elevated rhetoric and evocative gothic gloom, constructing an intricate nested narrative structure that moves smoothly from Walton's letters to Victor's confessions and into the creature's core developmental memoir. The language is heightened, formal, and steeped in poetic melancholy. Scenes such as the creature's awakening at "one in the morning" on that bleak, "dreary night in November" show an impeccable command of mood, deploying vivid, atmospheric details that linger long after reading. The elevated register lends a mythic quality to the dialogue, ensuring that both Victor and his creature articulate their suffering with solemnity and gravitas.

### Continuity

While the internal emotional logic remains consistent, the continuity of the plot relies on precarious logistical coincidences. The ease with which the creature masters high-register spoken and written French in a matter of months, combined with his implausible good fortune in finding the exact books and scientific logs necessary to understand his origin, challenges the work's otherwise coherent internal world. The narrative often bends physical plausibility to move characters into proximity, relying on fortunate encounters rather than deliberate detective work or logical trajectory.

### Scores

* **Character: 8/10** Victor's self-absorbed despair and the creature's eloquent suffering create a gripping, morally complex dialectic ("I was the true murderer").
* **Plot/Structure: 8/10** The Chinese-box nested epistolary structure ingeniously links Walton's polar quest with Victor's warning ("listen to my tale").
* **Pacing: 7/10** Narrative urgency pauses significantly for the detailed De Lacey education sequence, though the thematic enrichment justifies the detour.
* **Prose: 9/10** The prose achieves remarkable poetic resonance and brooding majesty, particularly in describing the creation on a "dreary night in November".
* **Continuity: 6/10** Key developments hinge heavily on convenient accidents, such as stumbling upon a "leathern portmanteau in the woods" loaded with perfect literary texts.
* **Overall: 8/10** A monumental philosophical tragedy whose chilling atmosphere and resonant questions far outweigh minor plotting conveniences.

### Final Verdict

A harrowing, deeply moving masterpiece of gothic science fiction that explores the devastation of intellect divorced from empathy. Despite minor narrative contrivances in plot delivery, its haunting character dynamics and atmospheric power remain unforgettable.

### Actionable Suggestions

1. **Ground Educational Materials Organically:** Integrate the creature's discovery of educational materials more organically into his environment rather than relying on chance discoveries. Instead of having him conveniently encounter a dropped "leathern portmanteau in the woods" containing classical literature and discovering Victor's exact laboratory notes in his taken clothes, have the creature salvage discarded school texts from town refuse or cottagers' explicit discards, improving narrative believability.
2. **Deepen Victor's Legal Resistance:** Deepen Victor's internal justification for remaining silent during Justine's trial beyond basic fear of being judged mad. Rather than allowing his passivity to read as unmitigated cowardice when facing the fear of "being thought insane," show Victor actively attempting indirect legal depositions or seeking secondary avenues of defense before resigning himself to paralysis.
